Core Theory: Foundations of Power and Elitism

You are trying to understand why power concentrates in the hands of a few, how elites reproduce themselves across generations, and what levers actually move Indian society. These foundational concepts give you the lens to see beneath surface-level political drama and recognise the deep structures at work.

Sanjaya Baru builds his analysis of India's power structure on a rich theoretical foundation that draws from both Western sociology and distinctly Indian frameworks. The book does not treat power as a simple matter of who holds office. Instead, it follows C. Wright Mills in seeing power as embedded in overlapping institutional hierarchies — the corporate boardroom, the bureaucratic secretariat, the party high command, and the media newsroom. To this, Baru adds the specifically Indian dimensions of caste, land, and the cultural authority of Lutyens' Delhi. The result is a layered account in which economic muscle, social status, political office, and cultural prestige all feed into one another, often reinforcing the same narrow circle.

What makes Baru's framework distinctive is his insistence that no single axis — not class, not caste, not political party — can explain the distribution of power in India. Each theoretical tradition illuminates a piece of the puzzle. Mills explains institutional convergence. Pareto and Mosca explain elite circulation and the rhetoric of renewal. Gramsci explains why the dominated sometimes consent to their own domination. Bourdieu explains how taste and education become weapons of exclusion. And Indian thinkers like Lohia and Kothari explain how caste and the Congress system created a uniquely Indian pattern of dominance. Taken together, these concepts form the intellectual backbone of the entire book.

The Power Elite — C. Wright Mills (1956)

C. Wright Mills argued that American society was dominated by a tight interlocking directorate of corporate executives, military brass, and political insiders — three hierarchies that had become increasingly interchangeable. These people moved between roles, sat on each other's boards, shared social circles, and developed a common worldview. Mills called this the power elite. Baru adopts this framework wholesale and maps it onto India, where the three hierarchies are business (corporate India, especially family-run conglomerates), the bureaucracy (particularly the IAS), and the political class (party leadership, ministers). To these, Baru adds a fourth: the cultural elite of Lutyens' Delhi — editors, columnists, public intellectuals, and celebrity artists who shape what is considered legitimate opinion. Mills observed that the power elite is not a conspiracy; it is a convergence of interest and outlook, reinforced by shared institutions and a sense of being the people who truly understand how the country works.

Power: A New Social Analysis — Bertrand Russell (1938)

Russell defined power as the production of intended effects — the capacity to make things happen in the world. He distinguished between different forms of power: priestly power, kingly power, naked power (violence), revolutionary power, and economic power. For Russell, power is the fundamental concept in social science, just as energy is in physics. Baru uses Russell's typology to analyse how different elite factions in India wield different kinds of power — the moral authority of the Gandhian, the coercive power of the state, the persuasive power of the media, and the transactional power of business. Russell's insight that power can be exercised through organisations, not just individuals, is central to understanding how the RSS (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, the Hindu nationalist volunteer organisation that is the ideological parent of the BJP) and the Congress Party built parallel power structures across Indian society.

Elite Circulation — Vilfredo Pareto and Gaetano Mosca

Pareto argued that history is a graveyard of aristocracies — elites do not last forever. They decay, lose their vigour, and are replaced by counter-elites who mobilise new social forces. This process, which he called the circulation of elites, is often dressed up as revolution or reform, but the underlying structure of elite rule remains. Mosca added that every ruling class justifies its power through a political formula — a legitimising myth that makes domination seem natural or divinely ordained. Baru uses these concepts to explain the decline of the Congress elite (the Nehru-Gandhi dynasty and its Anglophone, upper-caste inner circle) and the rise of the BJP's counter-elite under Narendra Modi — a group that built its legitimacy on the claim of representing the "real India" against a deracinated, Westernised establishment. The old elite's political formula was secularism and planned development; the new elite's formula is Hindu nationalism and aspirational capitalism. Pareto's framework helps Baru argue that what looked like a democratic upsurge was also, in significant measure, an elite circulation — one set of dominant castes and classes being displaced by another, with genuine social change happening in the interstices.

Hegemony and the Intellectuals — Antonio Gramsci

Gramsci's concept of hegemony describes how a ruling class maintains power not primarily through force but through the consent of the governed — a consent manufactured through institutions of civil society: schools, newspapers, churches, and cultural organisations. The organic intellectuals of a class are the thinkers, journalists, and educators who articulate its worldview and make it seem like common sense. Baru applies this to India by examining how the English-speaking elite — concentrated in Lutyens' Delhi, educated in a handful of institutions — defined what counted as "serious" policy debate and marginalised voices that did not speak its language. The "Khan Market Gang" (a shorthand for Delhi's upscale, Anglophone, liberal establishment centred around the high-end Khan Market shopping district) functioned, in this analysis, as the organic intellectuals of the old Congress elite. The rise of Hindi-language media, the proliferation of WhatsApp as a political communication tool, and the cultivation of a new set of public intellectuals by the Hindu nationalist movement represent, in Gramscian terms, the construction of a counter-hegemony. Baru is careful not to reduce everything to Gramsci — he notes that coercion and money still matter enormously — but the concept of hegemony helps explain why cultural battles over history textbooks, museum renovations, and the language of public discourse are so fiercely contested.

Cultural Capital and Distinction — Pierre Bourdieu

Bourdieu showed that social class is reproduced not just through money but through cultural capital — the tastes, manners, educational credentials, and aesthetic sensibilities that signal membership in the elite. His book Distinction demonstrated how the French bourgeoisie used subtle markers of taste to exclude outsiders and legitimise their own position. Baru finds this framework indispensable for understanding the role of English in India. English fluency is not just a practical skill; it is a gatekeeping mechanism that separates those who belong in the corridors of power from those who do not. The "English apartheid" that Kancha Ilaiah (an Indian political theorist and anti-caste activist) wrote about — and that Baru references — is a form of cultural capital that maps almost perfectly onto caste and class hierarchies. When Modi speaks in Hindi on the global stage, when the BJP promotes leaders who are more comfortable in regional languages, and when the new elite mocks the English affectations of the old guard, what is happening is a Bourdieu-style contest over which form of cultural capital will confer distinction. Baru notes that this is not simply a linguistic preference; it is a redistribution of symbolic power.

Caste, Class, and Dominance — M.N. Srinivas and Francine Frankel

No analysis of power in India can work without confronting caste. M.N. Srinivas introduced the concept of dominant caste — a caste that is numerically strong, controls land, and wields political influence in a given region. Francine Frankel extended this by showing how land reforms, the Green Revolution, and democratic politics created a new class of bullock capitalists — middle castes that moved from agricultural prosperity to political power. Baru synthesises these insights to argue that India's power elite cannot be understood through class alone. A wealthy Dalit (formerly "untouchable" caste, now Scheduled Caste) entrepreneur and a wealthy Bania (trader caste) industrialist may share economic interests, but they occupy very different positions in the social hierarchy. The caste composition of corporate boards, the judiciary, the upper bureaucracy, and the media remains heavily skewed toward upper castes, particularly Brahmins and Banias. Baru draws on D.L. Sheth's work to show that caste and class are not alternatives but intertwined axes of stratification — and that political mobilisation along caste lines has been one of the few effective ways for subordinate groups to challenge elite dominance.

Ram Manohar Lohia's Caste-Class Destruction

The socialist thinker Ram Manohar Lohia (1910–1967) argued that caste and class in India were twin pillars of oppression and that neither could be dismantled without attacking the other. His famous formulation identified the power structure as a coalition of "the high-caste rich" — Brahmins, Banias, and the English-educated — who together controlled the state, the economy, and the cultural sphere. Lohia's prescription was radical: reservation of jobs for backward castes, promotion of regional languages against English, and a politics that explicitly named caste as the central axis of Indian inequality. Baru treats Lohia as a pivotal figure because his ideas, dismissed as fringe during his lifetime, became the template for the Mandal Commission (which in 1990 recommended reservations for Other Backward Classes in government jobs) and the rise of OBC (Other Backward Classes) political parties. The "Lohia moment" in Indian politics — the assertion of backward-caste power from the 1970s onward — fundamentally disrupted the old Congress elite and created space for the BJP's later social engineering. Baru notes that Modi himself, an OBC by caste, represents in personal terms the political ascent that Lohia envisioned, even though the ideological vehicle is Hindu nationalism rather than socialism.

The Congress System — Rajni Kothari

Rajni Kothari's classic 1964 essay described the Congress Party not as a conventional political party but as a system — a grand coalition that accommodated diverse interests through internal factionalism and a network of patronage. The party acted as a sponge, absorbing dissent and co-opting challengers. Baru uses Kothari's framework to explain why the Congress dominated Indian politics for decades after independence: it was not just an electoral machine but a mechanism for managing elite competition. The "Congress system" worked by giving every significant social group — the landed upper castes, the emerging industrialists, the bureaucratic elite, the urban professionals — a stake in the status quo. Its decline, which Kothari began diagnosing as early as the late 1960s, was a slow unravelling of this coalition as new social forces — backward castes, regional parties, an assertive business class — demanded more than the system could accommodate. Baru treats the destruction of the Congress system as the necessary precondition for the emergence of a new power elite configuration under the BJP.

Intermediate Regimes — Michal Kalecki

The Polish economist Michal Kalecki developed the concept of intermediate regimes to describe post-colonial states where the domestic bourgeoisie was too weak to dominate and the working class too disorganised to challenge, leaving the state itself — controlled by a coalition of the petty bourgeoisie, the bureaucracy, and landed interests — as the primary actor. Baru applies this to Nehruvian India, arguing that the Indian state under Congress rule functioned as an intermediate regime: neither fully capitalist nor socialist, heavily reliant on the bureaucracy to allocate resources, and perpetually mediating between competing interest groups. The economic liberalisation of 1991 marked the end of the intermediate regime and the beginning of a more direct relationship between business and political power — a shift that Baru, who was involved in economic policy during that period, analyses in detail.

Institutions of Power

You need to map the actual institutional landscape — who sits where, what levers they control, and how different power centres interact. This section covers the concrete organisations and social groups that constitute India's power elite: business houses, landed families, the civil service, the military, intellectuals, and the media-celebrity complex.

Business and the State

Baru devotes substantial attention to the evolving relationship between Indian business and the state. The key transformation runs from the Bombay Plan of 1944-45 (a remarkable document in which leading industrialists — J.R.D. Tata, G.D. Birla, Kasturbhai Lalbhai, and others — sketched a blueprint for a planned, state-led economy) through the licence-permit raj of the Nehru-Indira years to the crony capitalism of the post-1991 era. Baru argues that Indian business has never been genuinely separate from the state; the question has always been which businesses get access and on what terms. The Bombay Plan represented an attempt by established industrial houses to shape the post-independence economic order and protect themselves from both foreign competition and domestic upstarts. It largely succeeded: the regulatory apparatus of the licence raj created high barriers to entry that benefited incumbent business families. Liberalisation in 1991 was supposed to dismantle this, but as Baru documents, it instead created new forms of state-business entanglement — spectrum allocations, land deals, mining licences, and election finance. The "crony capitalism" that critics decry is, in Baru's account, not an aberration but a structural feature of a political economy where the state remains the primary gatekeeper of economic opportunity.

HISTORICAL FRAMEWORK
The Bombay Plan (1944–45)
A blue-print for planned development authored by India's leading industrialists — and a masterclass in elite co-optation of state power.
What is actually happeningA small group of established business families — Tata, Birla, Lalbhai, Thapar, and others — pre-emptively shaped the economic policy framework of a soon-to-be-independent India. By endorsing planned development and a strong state role, they secured protection from foreign competition and ensured that the regulatory system would favour incumbents. The plan called for state-led industrialisation, land reforms (that they themselves would not be hurt by), and import substitution — all of which created a sheltered domestic market.
What it sounds like in practice“We believe that the state must play an active role in industrial development. Private enterprise alone cannot meet the nation's needs.” — spoken by an industrialist who knows that state licensing will keep new competitors out.
Payoff for the userDecades of protected markets, high barriers to entry for competitors, and a seat at the policy-making table. The Bombay Plan families dominated Indian industry well into the 1980s.
How to recognise it earlyWhen incumbent businesses advocate for "regulation" and "standards" that happen to be easier for them to meet than for smaller competitors. The language is always about national interest, never about market protection.
Success looks likeA regulatory architecture that the architects' grandchildren still benefit from. The Bombay Plan's imprint on Indian economic policy lasted nearly five decades.
Example phrase“A planned economy is not a socialist concession — it is, properly understood, the surest defence of domestic capital against foreign predation.”
STRUCTURAL PATTERN
Crony Capitalism: The Post-1991 Entanglement
Liberalisation dismantled the licence raj but created new, more lucrative points of state-business contact — spectrum, mines, land, and election finance.
What is actually happeningThe state no longer tells businesses what to produce, but it still controls access to natural resources, telecom spectrum, airport contracts, and land-use changes. Politicians need money for increasingly expensive elections; businesses need favourable regulatory decisions. The result is a revolving door of favours, facilitated by electoral bonds (introduced in 2018, allowing anonymous corporate donations to political parties), opaque bidding processes, and the strategic placement of family members in political office. Baru cites the work of Josy Joseph (The Feast of Vultures) and the essays in Jaffrelot, Kohli, and Murali's Business and Politics in India to document the scale of the problem.
What it looks like in practiceA telecom company gets spectrum on favourable terms; shortly afterward, its owner's relative is nominated to the Rajya Sabha (the upper house of India's Parliament, whose members are elected by state legislatures rather than directly by voters). A mining firm receives a sudden clearance after years of delay; the election cycle reveals large, anonymous donations to the ruling party.
Payoff for the userFor businesses: access to state-controlled resources on terms that competitors cannot match. For politicians: campaign funding and personal enrichment. The relationship is symbiotic and self-reinforcing.
Warning signalsSudden regulatory clearances after long delays; politicians' relatives appointed to corporate boards; unexplained wealth accumulation by public officials; electoral bond data showing disproportionate donations to the ruling party; the same business names appearing across multiple controversial allocations.
How to counterTransparency in political finance (making electoral bond donors public); competitive bidding for all natural resource allocations; strict conflict-of-interest rules for public officials; an independent and adequately staffed investigative apparatus. Baru notes that none of these are technically difficult — the obstacle is political will, since both major parties benefit from the current opacity.
Success looks likeA system where businesses compete on merit and efficiency rather than political connections, and where election outcomes are not determined by who raised more anonymous corporate cash.
Example phrase“The licence raj is dead. Long live the clearance raj — same gatekeepers, different gates, much larger sums of money.”
Watch out for: The argument that "all businesses lobby, so this is nothing new." Baru's point is precisely that the form of entanglement has changed — from petty corruption under the licence raj to mega-corruption after liberalisation, and the sums involved have grown exponentially.
OBSERVATION
The Rise of Regional Capitalists
Liberalisation did not just empower national business houses; it created space for regionally rooted entrepreneurs — often from non-traditional business castes — to challenge the old elite.
What is actually happeningBaru, drawing on his own earlier research and Harish Damodaran's India's New Capitalists, shows that post-1991 India saw the emergence of businessmen from castes that had never been in commerce — Kammas and Reddys in Andhra, Patidars in Gujarat, Marathas in Maharashtra. These regional capitalists built their fortunes in sectors like real estate, infrastructure, and regional media, and they forged direct relationships with state-level politicians. Their rise disrupted the old Bombay-Calcutta-Delhi business axis and created a more dispersed, federal structure of business power. Some of these regional players — like the Ambanis (originally from Gujarat) — became national giants. Others remained regional kingmakers, funding state elections and influencing local policy.
PayoffDiversification of the business elite beyond the traditional Bania (trader caste) and Parsi (Zoroastrian community that produced the Tatas and Godrejs) communities. More competition, but also more points of contact between business and political power at the state level.
How to recognise itLook at the caste composition of state-level business associations; track which businesses are the largest donors to regional parties; note which industrialists get appointed to state planning boards or nominated to the Rajya Sabha from particular states.
Example phrase“Delhi may set the macro policy, but the real action — the land conversions, the mining leases, the infrastructure contracts — happens in the state capitals. That is where the new money is being made.”

The Landed and the Feudals

Land remains a foundational source of power in India, and Baru traces the trajectory of landed elites from the zamindari (feudal landlord) system through post-independence land reforms (which often failed to dispossess the truly powerful) to the contemporary nexus of real estate, politics, and organised crime. The Green Revolution of the 1960s-70s created a class of prosperous middle-caste farmers — the bullock capitalists that Rudolph and Rudolph described and that Pradhan Prasad analysed as "kulaks" (rich peasants) — who translated agricultural wealth into political muscle, particularly in north India. Meanwhile, the former princely order did not vanish; princely families adapted to electoral politics, leveraging their residual land holdings, social prestige, and name recognition. Baru notes the persistence of "feudal" political dynasties across parties — families that have held parliamentary seats for generations, accumulating land and influence along the way. The Panama Papers (a 2016 leak of 11.5 million documents from the Panamanian law firm Mossack Fonseca exposing offshore financial dealings) and various land-deal scandals — involving figures like Robert Vadra (businessman and son-in-law of Sonia Gandhi) in Haryana and the Badal family in Punjab — illustrate how landed wealth and political power continue to intertwine in the 21st century.

SOCIAL TRANSFORMATION
Bullock Capitalists and Kulak Power
The Green Revolution created a new rural elite — middle castes with land, irrigation, and political ambition — that broke the old upper-caste, urban-dominated power structure.
What is actually happeningAgricultural prosperity from the Green Revolution (the introduction of high-yield seed varieties, fertilisers, and irrigation in the 1960s-70s) concentrated in the hands of middle castes — Jats in Haryana and western UP, Patidars in Gujarat, Marathas in Maharashtra, Kammas and Reddys in Andhra. These groups had the land, the access to credit, and the numerical strength to convert economic gains into political representation. They began demanding higher procurement prices for crops, cheaper inputs (fertiliser, electricity, water), and political reservations. Ashok Mitra's concept of terms of trade — the relative prices of agricultural versus industrial goods — became a political battleground, with kulak lobbies pushing for policies that favoured agriculture at the expense of urban consumers and industry.
What it sounds like“The farmer feeds the nation. Why should the city decide what his grain is worth?” — a political rallying cry that signals the assertion of rural economic power against urban policy elites.
PayoffMassive state investment in agricultural subsidies, loan waivers, and procurement prices. Political parties that ignored the farm lobby did so at their electoral peril. The kulak class became a decisive swing constituency in north Indian politics.
How to recognise itWatch for political demands framed as "protecting the farmer" that disproportionately benefit medium and large landholders rather than the landless or marginal farmers. The rhetoric is universal; the benefits are concentrated.
Success looks likeA political system where no major party can afford to ignore farm interests, and where agricultural policy is shaped as much by electoral calculations as by economic logic.
Example phrase“The Green Revolution was not just an agricultural transformation. It was a social revolution — one that turned the farmer into a political actor and the village into a constituency that Delhi could no longer ignore.”
ADAPTATION PATTERN
Princely Persistence: How Feudal Families Survived Democracy
The maharajas did not disappear in 1947; they adapted, entering electoral politics, leveraging residual land and name recognition to remain relevant.
What is actually happeningAfter the integration of princely states into the Indian Union (completed by 1949 under Sardar Patel) and the abolition of privy purses (the annual payments guaranteed to former rulers, abolished by Indira Gandhi in 1971), princely families pivoted to democratic politics. Many contested elections — some as independents, some under party banners — and won, drawing on residual loyalty, land holdings, and the glamour of royal lineage. Baru cites the example of Air India's branding (the erstwhile airline's maharaja mascot) as a symbol of how princely imagery was selectively incorporated into the national narrative. Even today, numerous MPs and MLAs come from former royal families. Their survival strategy is instructive: adapt the symbols of feudal authority to democratic contexts, convert land into campaign finance, and deploy inherited social capital as political currency.
Warning signalA candidate whose primary qualification is a royal surname, contesting from a constituency where their family once held sway. The feudal aura can mask a lack of programmatic commitments.
PayoffContinued relevance and political office for families that might otherwise have been consigned to history. Access to state resources in the present, built on inherited status from the past.
Example phrase“The throne is gone, but the name remains. And in a constituency where that name still means something, the ballot box can feel a lot like a durbar.”

Bureaucracy — The IAS and the Steel Frame

The Indian Administrative Service (IAS), successor to the colonial Indian Civil Service (ICS), was described by Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel (India's first Home Minister and Deputy Prime Minister) as the "steel frame" that held the country together. Baru examines how this cadre of generalist administrators accumulated immense power — over postings, licences, transfers, and the implementation of policy — and how that power has both persisted and been challenged in recent decades. The IAS is not a monolith; it is internally stratified by cadre (state of origin), by batch (year of entry), and by the network of patronage relationships each officer cultivates. The "transfer raj" — the use of frequent, politically motivated transfers to control and corrupt the bureaucracy — is a key mechanism through which politicians subdue the civil service. Baru references the N.N. Vohra Committee report (1993, on the criminalisation of politics and the nexus among criminals, politicians, and bureaucrats) to show how deeply the bureaucracy has been penetrated by the very forces it is supposed to regulate. Yet the IAS also retains significant structural power — its officers occupy the senior-most positions in every ministry, run the district administration, and control the flow of information to ministers. The Modi government's efforts to assert political control over the bureaucracy — through lateral appointments (bringing in outside experts rather than career civil servants), through centralised postings, and through public criticism of "obstructive" officials — represent a significant rebalancing of power between the political executive and the permanent civil service.

INSTITUTIONAL POWER
The IAS as Power Elite Constituent
The civil service is not merely an instrument of political power; it is itself a constituent of the power elite, with its own interests, internal hierarchies, and capacity for resistance.
What is actually happeningIAS officers control the implementation of every significant government decision. They write the cabinet notes, draft the legislation, issue the licences, and oversee the spending. This gives them enormous gatekeeping power. A determined bureaucrat can slow-walk a policy they oppose; a cooperative one can accelerate a dubious project. The service is self-reproducing — senior officers mentor juniors from their own cadre and batch, creating vertical patronage networks that span decades. Baru draws on S.K. Das's work on bureaucratic corruption and on P.S. Appu's critique of the decline of the All-India Services to argue that the IAS has simultaneously become more politically compliant and more internally corrupt — a deterioration that hollows out its institutional integrity while preserving its formal power.
What it looks likeA policy that is technically approved but never quite implemented. A file that moves in circles between departments. A regulatory clearance that takes exactly as long as the officer decides it should. The language is always procedural; the effect is substantive.
Payoff for the officerJob security, prestige, post-retirement sinecures (tribunals, commissions, regulatory bodies), and — in many cases — illicit income. The IAS remains one of the most sought-after careers in India because it offers a combination of power, stability, and social status that few other professions can match.
How to counter bureaucratic obstructionBaru notes that political leaders have several tools: frequent transfers (moving an uncooperative officer to a less sensitive post), lateral appointments (bringing in outside talent), and direct public pressure (naming and shaming through media). Each carries risks — transfers can be challenged in court, lateral appointments can breed resentment, and public criticism can demoralise the service.
Success looks likeA bureaucracy that is politically neutral but not politically oblivious — capable of giving honest advice and implementing decisions efficiently, without becoming either an obstructionist "deep state" or a supine instrument of political whim.
Example phrase“The steel frame has rusted in places, but it still holds the structure up. The question is whether you repair it, replace it, or let it corrode until something gives way.”
CONTROL MECHANISM
The Transfer Raj
Politicians use frequent, arbitrary transfers to discipline the bureaucracy — turning postings into rewards for loyalty and punishments for independence.
What is actually happeningAn IAS officer who refuses to accommodate a politician's request — a favourable land-use change, a rigged tender, a nixed investigation — finds herself transferred to a less desirable posting, often within months or even weeks. The threat of transfer is ever-present. This creates a powerful incentive for compliance. The system is not new — it dates back to the colonial era — but it has intensified as political competition has become more nakedly transactional. Baru cites Dan Banik's research on the "Transfer Raj" to document how the average tenure of a district magistrate or a departmental secretary has shrunk, making it nearly impossible for officers to develop expertise or exercise independent judgment.
Warning signalAn officer in a sensitive regulatory post is suddenly transferred after a controversial decision — or, equally telling, an officer known for pliability keeps getting prime postings. High churn rates in key positions are a red flag.
Payoff for the politicianA compliant bureaucracy that does not ask inconvenient questions. Favours can be routed through cooperative officers; investigations can be buried by transferring the investigator.
How to counterFixed minimum tenures for key posts, with transfers requiring the approval of an independent civil services board. The Supreme Court has endorsed this principle, but implementation remains patchy because state governments resist any constraint on their transfer powers.
Example phrase“You don't need to fire an honest officer. You just need to transfer him three times in two years. By the third posting, he will either quit or learn to keep his head down.”

The Military: The Absent Dialogue

Baru, drawing on Anit Mukherjee's The Absent Dialogue, argues that the Indian military is the weakest leg of the power elite tripod — institutionally powerful, professionally respected, but politically marginalised. Unlike in Pakistan or many other post-colonial states, the Indian military has never mounted a coup or directly intervened in politics. This is both a strength of Indian democracy and, Baru suggests, a source of dysfunction. Civil-military relations in India are characterised by what Mukherjee calls an "absent dialogue" — politicians and bureaucrats make strategic decisions with minimal military input, and the military leadership is structurally subordinated to a civilian bureaucracy (the Ministry of Defence, staffed by IAS officers) that often lacks operational expertise. Baru notes episodes — such as former Army Chief General V.K. Singh's public spat with the government over his age and his subsequent political career — that illustrate both the military's frustration with civilian control and the blurring of lines between military service and political ambition. Mills argued that in the United States, the military had become a full partner in the power elite; in India, Baru concludes, it remains an awkward, constrained, and sometimes resentful junior partner.

STRUCTURAL WEAKNESS
The Absent Dialogue in Civil-Military Relations
India's military is institutionally powerful but politically sidelined — a deliberate democratic choice that creates its own dysfunctions.
What is actually happeningStrategic decisions — defence procurement, force structure, operational doctrine — are made by civilian bureaucrats in the Ministry of Defence with limited substantive input from uniformed officers. The military is consulted but does not decide. The result is often procurement delays, mismatched equipment, and strategic plans that look good on paper but lack operational grounding. The military's frustration with this arrangement occasionally surfaces in public — General V.K. Singh's "presstitutes" remark (a crude coinage criticising media complicity) and his later political career being notable examples.
Payoff for civilian controlThe system prevents the emergence of a politically ambitious military leadership. India has never faced a coup threat — a genuine achievement given the region's history. The cost, however, is strategic sub-optimality.
Warning signalRetired generals entering politics (V.K. Singh joined the BJP and became a minister); serving chiefs making public statements on political matters; procurement scandals that suggest the civilian bureaucracy is both unaccountable and incompetent.
Success looks likeA civil-military relationship where the military has a meaningful voice in strategic decisions, civilian oversight is informed and competent, and the boundary between military service and political ambition is clearly respected.
Example phrase“The Indian soldier is respected. The Indian general is consulted. But the Indian military is not a full partner in the power elite — and that is both a democratic triumph and a strategic liability.”

Policy and Public Intellectuals

Baru examines the role of public intellectuals — economists, historians, political commentators, and policy analysts — as both legitimisers of elite power and, occasionally, its critics. The Nehruvian state actively cultivated a class of Western-trained economists and social scientists who staffed the Planning Commission, the universities, and the research institutes. Figures like John Maynard Keynes (through his influence on Indian economic thinking) and later Indian economists like K.N. Raj and Sukhamoy Chakravarty shaped policy from within the establishment. Baru notes that the "public intellectual" in India has historically been an English-speaking, upper-caste, left-of-centre figure — the very profile that the "Khan Market Gang" represents. Romila Thapar (the eminent historian) and Mukul Kesavan (the writer and essayist) are discussed as exemplars of this tradition. The rise of Hindu nationalist intellectual infrastructure — think tanks, online magazines, and social media networks — represents a deliberate effort to build a counter-establishment of organic intellectuals in the Gramscian sense. Baru treats this as a healthy development in one respect (intellectual competition) and a worrying one in another (the instrumentalisation of scholarship for political ends). The key question he poses is whether India's public intellectuals serve as independent critics of power or as sophisticated apologists for whichever faction they are aligned with.

GRAMSCIAN APPLICATION
Organic Intellectuals and the Battle for Legitimacy
Every aspiring ruling group needs intellectuals who can articulate its worldview as common sense — and India is witnessing a fierce competition between two intellectual ecosystems.
What is actually happeningThe old Congress elite cultivated a network of English-language intellectuals — economists at the Delhi School of Economics, historians at JNU (Jawaharlal Nehru University), columnists at the English-language press — who defined the terms of respectable policy debate. Their assumptions (secularism, socialism, non-alignment, the primacy of the Centre) became the default setting of Indian public discourse. The BJP and the RSS spent decades building a parallel intellectual infrastructure — the Deendayal Research Institute, the India Foundation, a network of Hindi and regional-language publications — that challenged every one of those assumptions. The battle is not just over policy but over who gets to define what is reasonable. When a BJP spokesperson dismisses a critical article as "Lutyens' media" or "Khan Market journalism," the subtext is Gramscian: your intellectual authority is not universal; it is the class-bound worldview of a discredited elite.
What it sounds like“These are the same people who have been running this country for seventy years. They have never visited a village. They think India ends at the Yamuna. Why should they get to tell us what is good for the nation?”
PayoffWhoever wins the battle of ideas also wins the policy space. A government that can delegitimise its intellectual critics does not need to answer them — it can simply dismiss them as out of touch.
Warning signalWhen every critic is dismissed as "elite," "anti-national," or "Westernised" without engagement with the substance of the criticism. This is the intellectual equivalent of an ad hominem attack — effective politically, corrosive to genuine debate.
How to counterEngage substantively, in languages and on platforms that reach beyond the English-speaking bubble. Baru's implicit advice is that the old intellectual elite lost ground partly because it stopped speaking to — or even trying to understand — the India outside its own circles.
Example phrase“The Nehruvian intellectual was the organic intellectual of the Congress system. The question now is whether the BJP can produce organic intellectuals of comparable depth — or whether it will rely on the easier route of simply delegitimising its critics.”

Opinion Makers and Celebrities

C. Wright Mills included "celebrities" and media figures as a secondary stratum of the power elite — not the core decision-makers, but the people who shape public perceptions of legitimacy and create the cultural atmosphere in which power is exercised. Baru extends this to India, examining the role of newspaper editors, television anchors, Bollywood stars, and cricket icons. During the Emergency (1975–77, when Indira Gandhi suspended civil liberties and ruled by decree), India Today magazine documented how the press was cowed and how some prominent figures collaborated while others resisted. More recently, the concentration of media ownership in the hands of a few industrial houses — many with direct business interests affected by government policy — has raised questions about editorial independence. Baru notes that celebrity endorsements of political leaders, the use of social media influencers for propaganda, and the blurring of lines between journalism and activism are all features of an Indian public sphere that is more vibrant but also more manipulated than ever. The power of opinion makers lies not in formal authority but in their capacity to shape what Mills called "the unevaluated backdrop of everyday life" — the assumptions people carry about who is credible, what is normal, and which voices deserve to be heard.

MILLS EXTENSION
Opinion Shapers as the Secondary Elite
Editors, anchors, and celebrities do not issue orders — but they define the boundaries of acceptable opinion, and that is a form of power no less real for being diffuse.
What is actually happeningMedia owners are increasingly drawn from the same business class whose interests are directly affected by government decisions. A corporate house that owns a television network and also has telecom, infrastructure, or defence contracts faces an obvious conflict: critical coverage of the government could jeopardise those contracts. Even when individual journalists are professionally committed to independence, the structural pressure is toward conformity. Meanwhile, celebrities — film stars, cricketers — lend their goodwill to political parties, sometimes transitioning into formal political roles (as with several Bollywood actors who became MPs). Their power is the power to make a political brand feel aspirational rather than transactional.
Warning signalA media house that never criticises the government of the day, or that criticises only in ways that do not threaten its owners' business interests. A celebrity whose political statements are indistinguishable from a party press release.
How to counterMedia literacy: understanding who owns which outlet and what interests they hold. Diversifying information sources. Supporting independent, reader-funded journalism. Baru is not prescriptive here, but the implication is clear — a citizenry that cannot map the ownership structure of its information diet cannot effectively participate in democracy.
Example phrase“The editor does not need to take orders from the owner. She just needs to know, without being told, which stories will cause trouble and which will not. That is not censorship. That is conditioning — and it is more effective than any censor could hope to be.”

Power Dynamics and Shifts

You are trying to understand the big transformations — how one elite displaces another, what tactics they use, and what the consequences are for the rest of society. This section covers Modi's rhetorical strategy, the symbolic siege of Lutyens' Delhi, the long arc of political change from Congress dominance to BJP ascendancy, the phenomenon of the wealthy opting out of the national project, and the rebalancing of power that is still underway.

Modi's Metaphors: The Rhetoric of Elite Displacement

Baru devotes an entire chapter to the metaphors and rhetorical strategies that Narendra Modi has used to challenge the old elite. Modi's language is not incidental to his political project; it is central to it. When he speaks of "Lutyens' Delhi" — referring to the colonial-era architectural zone designed by Edwin Lutyens that houses India's Parliament, ministries, and elite residences — he is invoking a whole set of associations: English-speaking, upper-caste, out-of-touch, condescending, morally decadent. The "Khan Market Gang" serves a similar function — a shorthand for the media-intellectual-bureaucratic nexus that, in Modi's framing, has run India for its own benefit while ignoring the "real" India of small towns and villages. Baru analyses how Modi's metaphors work: they are specific enough to be evocative (Khan Market is a real place, with real power dynamics) but vague enough to be elastic (any critic can be branded as part of the "gang"). The "chaiwala" (tea-seller) narrative that Modi himself cultivated — his humble origins as a contrast to the dynastic privilege of the Gandhis — is another metaphor, this one of authenticity and aspiration. Baru treats these metaphors not as cheap rhetoric but as effective political communication that restructured the terms of Indian political debate. The old elite found itself on the defensive, having to prove it was not "anti-national" or "elitist" — a burden it was not accustomed to bearing.

RHETORICAL TACTIC
Lutyens' Delhi as Political Shorthand
A geographical reference — the colonial-era administrative core of New Delhi — becomes a devastating metonym for everything the aspirational outsider resents about the establishment.
What is actually happeningModi weaponises a place-name. "Lutyens' Delhi" conjures images of manicured lawns, private club memberships, English conversations, and a self-perpetuating elite that networks at book launches and dinner parties while remaining utterly disconnected from the lives of ordinary Indians. The charge is not that this elite is corrupt (though that is sometimes implied); it is that it is illegitimate — it does not represent the nation, it merely occupies its commanding heights. This framing allows Modi to position himself as the outsider storming the citadel, even when he is, in fact, the Prime Minister with more concentrated power than any predecessor in decades.
What it sounds like“I could not win over Lutyens' Delhi,” Modi said in 2019 — a statement that is simultaneously a confession, a boast, and a campaign slogan. It tells his supporters: the people who matter most despise me, and that is exactly why you should trust me.
PayoffDelegitimisation of elite criticism. Any negative coverage can be dismissed as the sour grapes of a displaced establishment. The tactic also builds solidarity with supporters who have themselves felt looked down upon by the English-speaking elite.
How to recognise itWhen a specific geographical or institutional reference (Lutyens' Delhi, Khan Market, JNU, the "English media") is used as a pejorative shorthand that ends rather than begins an argument. The function is to label, not to analyse.
How to respondBaru's implicit counsel is to refuse the frame without being defensive. Acknowledge that elites exist and have often failed the country, but insist on the distinction between criticising an elite and dismissing all criticism as elite. The response must also engage in the languages and on the platforms that reach beyond the Lutyens' bubble — otherwise, it merely confirms the charge.
Example phrase“Lutyens' Delhi is not a place. It is a state of mind — and the Prime Minister has successfully made it the villain in a national melodrama where he plays the hero from the hinterland.”
Paired with: Khan Market Gang — the media-intellectual variant of the same tactic.
RHETORICAL TACTIC
The "Khan Market Gang"
A dismissive label for the media-intellectual elite — journalists, columnists, think-tankers, and policy analysts — whose social hub is an upscale Delhi shopping district.
What is actually happeningKhan Market is one of the most expensive retail locations in India, frequented by journalists, diplomats, lawyers, and bureaucrats. By naming the elite after its favourite shopping destination, Modi's camp turns a lifestyle into an indictment. The "Khan Market Gang" is accused of being insular, condescending, and — crucially — politically irrelevant outside its own echo chamber. The label serves to neutralise critical journalism by pre-emptively discrediting the critic. Raj Kamal Jha and Ravish Tiwari's 2019 Indian Express interview with Modi captures the PM's own use of the phrase, disavowing its importance while simultaneously reinforcing its sting.
PayoffMedia criticism that might once have shaped public opinion now bounces off a base that has been taught to see the critic as part of a despised out-group. The tactic works even when the criticism is factually sound — the source has been pre-invalidated.
Warning signalDismissal of any unfavourable report or column with a label rather than a substantive rebuttal. The goal is to make the audience stop listening before they have even heard the argument.
Counter-tacticReport from outside the bubble. Build credibility with audiences that are not already predisposed to trust you. Avoid the temptation to sneer — nothing confirms the "Khan Market" stereotype faster than a columnist who sounds like he thinks his readers are beneath him.
Example phrase“The Khan Market Gang hasn't created my image. Forty-five years of tapasya (disciplined effort) has. You cannot dismantle it.” — Narendra Modi, 2019.

POLITICAL POSTURE
The Outsider as Insider: Modi's Double Game
Modi simultaneously runs the most powerful government in decades and campaigns as the victim of an entrenched establishment — a paradox that is, in fact, the strategy.
What is actually happeningEven after winning two landslide majorities, controlling the bulk of India's state governments, and presiding over a centralisation of power unseen since Indira Gandhi, Modi continues to invoke the language of the persecuted outsider. The "establishment" is not a structure he now leads but a shadowy cabal that still resists him — in the courts, in the media, in the universities, in the "deep state." This posture serves multiple functions: it rallies the base, it justifies any failure as the result of elite sabotage, and it keeps the BJP's cadre in a permanent state of mobilisation. Baru notes the irony: the most powerful man in India speaks as though he is still storming the barricades, not occupying the palace.
Warning signalWhen a government with a parliamentary majority, control of most states, and a pliant media environment still claims to be besieged by "forces" that it cannot quite name. This is not a description of reality; it is a mobilisation device.
PayoffPerpetual campaign mode. The base never rests. The cadre never demobilises. And inconvenient facts — economic slowdown, joblessness, social conflict — can always be blamed on the "elite" that is supposedly still pulling strings behind the scenes.
How to counterInsist on accountability for outcomes, not intentions. The question is not whether the elite is obstructive; it is what the government has actually delivered. Baru's approach is to grant the rhetorical power of the outsider narrative while insisting that governance must be judged on results.
Example phrase“He governs from the fortress but speaks from the trenches. It is a remarkable political feat — and one that makes it very difficult to hold him to account for what happens inside the walls.”

The Siege of Lutyens' Delhi

Baru treats the physical and symbolic transformation of Lutyens' Delhi — the planned imperial capital built by the British architect Edwin Lutyens — as a proxy for the larger battle over who owns the idea of India. The Modi government's decision to build a new Parliament building, the renaming of streets, the proposed redevelopment of the Central Vista (the ceremonial boulevard from Rashtrapati Bhavan to India Gate), and the sidelining of institutions like the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library are all acts of symbolic reclamation. Anish Kapoor (the British-Indian sculptor) and other critics have denounced the Central Vista project as a "fascist" erasure of heritage; supporters see it as a long-overdue assertion of Indian identity over colonial architecture. Baru is more measured, treating the battle over Delhi's built environment as a concrete manifestation of the elite circulation Pareto described — the new elite wants its own monuments, its own architectural vocabulary, its own spatial representation of power. The old elite's attachment to Lutyens' Delhi is, in this reading, not just aesthetic but political — those colonnades and manicured gardens were the stage on which the Congress elite performed its authority for seven decades. To redesign the stage is to signal that a new drama is being performed.

SYMBOLIC CONTEST
The Central Vista and the Politics of Architecture
The redevelopment of Delhi's ceremonial core is not about urban planning — it is about which India gets to inscribe its values in stone.
What is actually happeningThe Modi government's Central Vista project — a wholesale redevelopment of the parliamentary precinct, including a new Parliament building — is the most ambitious physical transformation of Lutyens' Delhi since independence. Critics, including the artist Anish Kapoor and the architect Gautam Bhatia, have charged that the project is being rammed through without adequate consultation, that it erases heritage, and that it represents a majoritarian assertion of Hindu nationalist aesthetics. Supporters counter that the old Parliament building was inadequate for a growing legislature and that a sovereign nation should not be bound by colonial-era architecture. Baru's interest is in what the controversy reveals about elite competition: who gets to decide what the nation's capital looks like is, in a very direct sense, who gets to define what the nation is.
What it sounds like“We are building a Parliament that reflects the soul of India, not the ego of a colonial architect.” — the government's framing. Versus: “This is not renovation; this is erasure.” — the critics' framing.
Payoff for the governmentA visible, irreversible assertion of the new elite's permanence. Buildings last longer than governments. The Central Vista will be the BJP's architectural legacy long after individual politicians have retired.
Warning signalMajor architectural projects pushed through with minimal public consultation, during a pandemic or other crisis that limits scrutiny. The pattern is not unique to India — regimes everywhere use monumental architecture to assert their permanence.
Example phrase“Lutyens designed a capital for an empire. The question now is whether the republic that replaced it wants to preserve that gesture or overwrite it. Either choice is political.”
Dependency: This symbolic contest only makes sense if you understand the Gramscian and Bourdieu-style dynamics of cultural capital. The buildings matter because they are sites where legitimacy is produced and contested, not merely because they are functional structures.

Shifts in Political Power: From Congress System to BJP Dominance

Baru traces the arc of political power in independent India through several phases: the Congress system (1947–67), its decline and the Emergency interlude (1967–77), the era of coalition politics and regional assertion (1977–2014), and the return of single-party dominance under the BJP (2014–present). Each phase involved a reconfiguration of the relationship between caste, class, and political representation. The Emergency (1975–77) was, in Baru's analysis, both the high-water mark of Congress authoritarianism and the catalyst for its eventual disintegration — it united the opposition, energised civil society, and discredited the Gandhian-Nehruvian claim to moral superiority. The Mandal Commission's implementation in 1990 — which extended job reservations to Other Backward Classes (OBCs) — was a seismic event that fractured the old upper-caste consensus and created the electoral arithmetic for OBC-led parties to challenge Congress dominance. The BJP's genius, Baru argues, was to build an upper-caste/backward-caste coalition that combined Mandal (social justice for OBCs) with Kamandal (Hindu nationalist cultural politics) — a formula that allowed it to appeal to both caste aspiration and religious identity. The criminalisation of politics, analysed extensively by Milan Vaishnav in When Crime Pays, is treated by Baru as a symptom of deeper structural changes: as elections became more expensive and more competitive, parties turned to candidates who could self-finance and — when necessary — deploy muscle. The result is a Parliament where a significant percentage of MPs face criminal charges, a fact that Baru presents without sensationalism, as a feature of the current political economy rather than an aberration.

HISTORICAL TURNING POINT
The Emergency as Elite Rupture (1975–77)
Indira Gandhi's suspension of democracy broke the moral legitimacy of the Congress elite and created the conditions for its long-term decline.
What is actually happeningFacing a court judgment that would have disqualified her from office, Indira Gandhi declared a national emergency — suspending civil liberties, jailing opposition leaders, censoring the press, and ruling by decree. Baru treats the Emergency not just as an authoritarian episode but as a rupture in the elite consensus that had sustained the Congress system. The Emergency showed that the Congress elite was willing to destroy democratic institutions to preserve its own power. When elections were finally held in 1977, the opposition — united for the first time under the Janata Party banner — won a decisive victory. Though Congress returned to power in 1980, the spell was broken. The party never fully recovered its moral authority or its organisational coherence.
Payoff at the timeShort-term survival for Indira Gandhi. Long-term delegitimisation of the Congress brand. The Emergency became the ultimate symbol of what the Congress elite would do when its power was threatened — a reference point that the BJP still invokes decades later.
Warning signal then and nowA leader facing legal or electoral jeopardy who begins to frame the crisis as a threat to national stability rather than a personal predicament. The conflation of personal survival with national interest is the tell.
Example phrase“The Emergency was the Congress elite's true face — revealed when the mask of democratic propriety no longer served its purpose.”
STRATEGIC SYNTHESIS
Mandal + Kamandal: The BJP's Coalition Formula
The BJP's electoral dominance rests on a fusion of caste-based social justice (Mandal) and Hindu nationalist identity politics (Kamandal) — a formula that took decades to perfect.
What is actually happeningThe Mandal Commission's recommendation of 27% job reservations for OBCs, implemented in 1990 by the V.P. Singh government, threatened to split the Hindu vote along caste lines. Upper castes were alarmed; OBCs were energised. The BJP's response was not to oppose Mandal — which would have alienated OBC voters — but to supplement it with the Ram Janmabhoomi movement (the campaign to build a temple at the disputed site in Ayodhya, believed by Hindus to be the birthplace of Lord Ram), which united Hindus across caste lines around a religious identity. This dual strategy — Mandal for the backward castes, Kamandal (the saffron flag of Hindu nationalism) for the upper castes — allowed the BJP to build a cross-caste coalition that the explicitly caste-based parties could not match. Baru credits the BJP's strategists with understanding something that the socialists and the Congress both missed: that caste identity and religious identity are not mutually exclusive and that a skilled political entrepreneur can activate either or both depending on the electoral context.
PayoffA durable electoral coalition that spans upper castes (who provide ideological cadre and financial resources) and OBCs (who provide numerical strength). Modi, an OBC himself, is the living embodiment of this synthesis — a backward-caste leader of an upper-caste-dominated party.
How to recognise itWhen a party simultaneously appeals to caste identity (fielding OBC candidates, promising OBC reservation protections) and religious identity (temple construction, anti-conversion laws, cow protection). The two appeals are not contradictory; they are complementary, targeting different segments of the same coalition.
Example phrase“Mandal gave the backward castes self-respect. Kamandal gave them a place in a larger Hindu nation. The BJP's genius was to realise that most Indians wanted both — and that no party before it had offered them together.”
STRUCTURAL SYMPTOM
The Criminalisation of Politics
When elections become expensive and the state controls lucrative rents, parties turn to candidates who can self-finance and self-protect — and that often means candidates with criminal resources.
What is actually happeningBaru draws on Milan Vaishnav's When Crime Pays and the work of the Association for Democratic Reforms to document a startling fact: a significant percentage of Indian MPs and MLAs face criminal charges, including serious offences like murder, kidnapping, and extortion. This is not because voters are ignorant or indifferent; Vaishnav's research shows that voters often knowingly choose candidates with criminal records because those candidates are seen as "effective" — able to get things done in a system where legality is an obstacle, not a pathway. The criminal-politician is also attractive to parties because he brings his own financing and his own enforcement apparatus, reducing the party's costs. The Vohra Committee (1993) officially acknowledged the nexus between criminals, politicians, and bureaucrats, but little has been done to break it.
Warning signalA candidate whose primary qualification is "access" — the ability to make a phone call and get a file moved, a rival intimidated, a contract awarded. When "effectiveness" is the pitch, ask what kind of effectiveness is being offered and at whose expense.
How to counterBaru is not optimistic about easy fixes. Fast-track courts for cases against politicians, stricter disclosure requirements, and public pressure can help at the margins. But the underlying driver — the cost of elections and the role of the state in allocating economic opportunity — is structural. Until elections become cheaper or the state's gatekeeping role shrinks, the criminal-politician will remain a feature of Indian democracy.
Example phrase“The voter is not stupid. She knows her candidate has a criminal record. She also knows that the system is stacked against her, and a 'strongman' — literally — may be the only one who can break through it on her behalf. The rot is in the system, not in the voter.”
Watch out for: The temptation to moralise. Baru's point is that criminalisation is a rational adaptation to a dysfunctional system. Condemning voters for choosing criminal candidates misses the structural incentives that make such choices sensible.

The Secession of the Successful

Robert Reich's 1991 essay "Secession of the Successful" described how the American wealthy were withdrawing from public goods — sending their children to private schools, living in gated communities, using private security — and thereby losing their stake in the quality of public institutions. Baru applies this concept to India's globalised elite. India has one of the world's largest diasporas — over 17 million people of Indian origin living abroad — and the wealthiest Indians are disproportionately likely to send their children overseas for education, to invest in foreign real estate, and to hold foreign passports or residency permits. This "secession" is not just physical; it is psychological. The globally mobile Indian elite has, Baru argues, a diminished stake in the quality of Indian public goods — if the local school is bad, their children are at boarding school in England; if the local hospital is inadequate, they fly to Singapore for treatment. This withdrawal weakens the pressure for public improvement and creates a bifurcated society where the successful are less and less connected to the fate of their fellow citizens. Baru connects this to the phenomenon of "brain drain" — the exodus of top talent to Western countries — and to its partial reversal as "brain gain" when some of that talent returns with capital and networks. But the net effect, he suggests, is that the Indian elite is more globally integrated and less nationally anchored than at any point in modern history.

REICH'S FRAMEWORK APPLIED
The Secession of the Successful — Indian Edition
When the wealthy can opt out of public goods, they lose the incentive to fight for their improvement — and the public sphere deteriorates for everyone else.
What is actually happeningIndia's richest families increasingly live in a parallel universe: private security, international schools, medical treatment abroad, second passports, foreign property, and social circles that are global rather than local. The children of the elite study at universities in the US, UK, and Australia and often do not return. Even those who remain in India are buffered from the consequences of state failure in ways that ordinary citizens are not. This "secession" is not a conspiracy; it is the aggregate effect of individual rational choices. But its collective consequence is a ruling class that has less and less skin in the game of Indian governance. Baru cites the Indian Express investigation showing that over half of board toppers from twenty years ago now live abroad — a vivid illustration of where the country's best-educated talent ends up.
What it sounds like“Of course we love India. We come back every winter. But the children are in Boston now, and honestly, the air quality here...” — the tone is apologetic, but the underlying calculus is clear: the family's future is being hedged.
Payoff for the individualBetter education, healthcare, and security for the family. Global opportunities. A diversified life portfolio that reduces dependence on any single country's fortunes.
Societal costDrain of talent, capital, and — crucially — attention. An elite that is psychologically semi-detached from the nation is less likely to invest its energies in national improvement. The public sphere loses its most powerful advocates.
How to recognise itThe "just in case" foreign residency permit. The children's education planned entirely around foreign universities. The casual assumption that serious medical treatment means flying to Singapore or London. Each choice is individually defensible; the pattern is what matters.
Counter-argumentBaru notes that the diaspora also sends remittances, invests in Indian startups, and lobbies for Indian interests abroad. The "brain drain" has a "brain gain" dimension, especially in technology. The question is one of balance — and Baru's worry is that the balance has tipped too far toward exit and too little toward voice.
Example phrase“The wealthiest Indians have more in common with their counterparts in London and New York than with their own countrymen living a few kilometres from the homes they still nominally occupy.”
Dependency: This concept only makes sense once you understand that elites are defined not just by what they control but by what they depend on. The seceding elite reduces its dependence on Indian public goods, and that changes its relationship to the Indian state.

A Changing Balance of Power

The final chapter of Baru's analysis brings the threads together. India's power elite is being reconfigured along multiple axes simultaneously: caste (the rise of OBCs and the relative decline of upper-caste dominance), class (the emergence of new business groups and the secession of the globalised wealthy), region (the shift of economic dynamism from the old Bombay-Calcutta-Delhi triangle to new growth centres in the south and west), and ideology (the displacement of secular nationalism by Hindu nationalism as the legitimising formula of the state). Baru is careful not to present this as a simple story of progress or decline. Some changes are genuine democratisation — the entry of previously excluded groups into the power structure. Others are merely elite circulation — one set of dominant groups replacing another without fundamentally changing the structure of dominance. And some are worrying — the weakening of institutions, the criminalisation of politics, the secession of the globally mobile, the hollowing out of intellectual independence. Baru's overall prescription, developed in the closing section, is for a more self-aware elite — one that understands its own composition, acknowledges its own interests, and takes seriously its obligations to the public goods on which a functional democracy depends.

SYNTHESIS
Elite Circulation, Indian Style
What looks like democratic revolution — new faces, new parties, new rhetoric — is often, at a deeper level, the replacement of one elite coalition by another, with genuine social transformation happening in the gaps.
What is actually happeningThe BJP's rise is simultaneously a democratic broadening (OBCs, regional aspirants, small-town India gaining political representation) and an elite circulation (upper-caste ideological dominance continuing, business interests consolidating, the old intellectual elite being replaced by a new one). Pareto would recognise the pattern: the old elite has lost its vigour and its legitimising formula; a counter-elite has mobilised new social forces and captured the state. But Mosca would note that the new elite still needs a political formula — and Hindu nationalism, with its claims to represent the authentic nation against a deracinated pseudo-elite, serves that function admirably. The test, Baru suggests, is whether the circulation of elites also produces a circulation of outcomes — whether the lives of ordinary Indians improve, whether institutions grow stronger, whether power becomes more accountable — or whether it is simply new names on the same old structures.
Payoff for understandingThe ability to see past the noise of political competition to the deeper continuities. You stop being surprised by each new scandal or each new government because you understand the structural forces that produce them.
How to recognise it in real timeLook at the caste, class, and educational background of the new power holders. Are they genuinely different from their predecessors, or are they the same social types with different party affiliations? Look at policy outcomes, not just political rhetoric. Who actually benefits from the new dispensation?
Example phrase“India has changed its rulers. Whether it has changed its ruling structure — the interlocking hierarchies of caste, capital, and cultural authority — is a question that will take another generation to answer.”
CONCEPT CLARIFICATION
The "Hindu Rate of Growth" — Misunderstood and Reclaimed
Raj Krishna's famous phrase is often read as a racial dismissal of India's economic potential. Baru, citing his father's original essay, offers a more nuanced reading — one rooted in a Hindu philosophical acceptance of gradualism.
What is actually happeningThe term "Hindu rate of growth" was coined by economist Raj Krishna, drawing on an essay by B.P.R. Vithal (Baru's father) published under the pseudonym "Najinyanupi" — a Lakota word meaning "surrounded." Vithal's argument was not that Hinduism condemned India to slow growth but that the Hindu worldview — which compares each individual's present to their own past rather than to the neighbour next door, and which subsumes individual identity within community — allows a society to sustain itself even at low rates of change. The gradualness of change and the resilience of mind and spirit, Vithal argued, explained India's political stability despite deep inequality. The term was later misinterpreted as a pejorative suggesting that a "Hindu" India was incapable of high growth. Baru reclaims the original meaning to make a subtle point: India's resilience is its strength, but resilience can also become complacency — an acceptance of mediocrity dressed up as spiritual equanimity.
What it sounds like (misused)“India is doomed to grow at 3.5% because of its Hindu culture.” — a reductionist and frankly Orientalist reading that Baru explicitly rejects.
What it sounds like (original intent)“India endures. Change comes slowly, but it comes, and the society does not shatter under the strain of its own contradictions. That is not a weakness; it is a particular kind of strength — but it can also be an excuse for inaction.”
Example phrase“The Hindu rate of growth was never about Hinduism as a drag on development. It was about the Hindu capacity to sustain social order at low rates of material progress — a capacity that is both a civilisation's gift and a reformer's frustration.”
Watch out for: Using the term as an ethnic stereotype. Baru's excavation of its origins is an antidote to that lazy usage — and a reminder that concepts travel and mutate in ways their authors never intended.

What the Author Wants You to Walk Away Doing Differently

Sanjaya Baru does not offer a ten-point programme or a manifesto. His prescription is analytical rather than programmatic, and it is directed at multiple audiences. For the citizen-reader, the injunction is to see clearly — to understand that power in India is not chaotic but structured, that the structures are legible, and that understanding them is the precondition for changing them. For the old elite — the Lutyens' Delhi establishment that Baru himself, as a former media advisor to Prime Minister Manmohan Singh and a longtime economic journalist, is arguably part of — the message is uncomfortable: you lost because you stopped listening. You confused your own social circle with the nation. You spoke only to each other, in a language most Indians do not speak. If you want relevance, you must earn it by engaging with the India that actually exists, not the one you imagine from your salon. For the new elite — the BJP establishment and its allied business and intellectual networks — the warning is against hubris. Displacing an old elite does not automatically make you more legitimate; it just makes you the new incumbent. The structural problems Baru identifies — crony capitalism, the criminalisation of politics, the hollowing out of institutions, the secession of the successful — will not solve themselves simply because a different party is in power. A new elite that merely replicates the old elite's vices while reversing its virtues is not progress; it is just a changing of the guard. Baru's deepest commitment, discernible on every page, is to the idea that power must be analysed without sentimentality — but that analysis is wasted if it does not serve the purpose of holding power accountable to those over whom it is exercised.

If there is a single sentence that captures Baru's stance, it is this: India's power elite has changed, but the structures that produce elite power have not changed nearly enough — and a democracy that does not continuously scrutinise those structures is a democracy in name only.
